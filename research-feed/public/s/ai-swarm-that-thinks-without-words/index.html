AI agents

A swarm of small AIs that think without words

A new method lets hundreds of small AIs share ideas as numbers, not sentences. It is fast. It does not win every puzzle.

Hook · 1 of 14

What if an AI could think without words?

You solve a hard puzzle in your head.

You do not say each step out loud.

What if an AI could work the same way?

2 of 14

Today's AI helpers write out every thought

Search tools today let an AI write long notes to itself before each try.

Each try is a small program that attacks a math puzzle.

Writing those notes eats 20 to 35 seconds for every program tried.

Source: paper, section 4.1

Question · 3 of 14

What if the AI skipped the words?

Words are slow. Numbers inside the AI are fast.

Could many tiny thinkers work in that fast, wordless space?

Animation · 4 of 14

One writer against a swarm

On the left, one thinker writes every step.

5 of 14

A swarm that thinks in numbers

SwarmEvolve runs 450 small agents in each round.

Each agent thinks inside the AI's hidden numbers, without writing words.

They pass the AI's short-term memory to each other.

A filter keeps the ideas that sit furthest apart.

One summarizer reads all those notes and produces a fresh program.

Source: paper, section 4.1; section 1

6 of 14

The test

The team set 21 puzzles in maths and science.

Each method got 60 minutes on one graphics card.

Three rival methods competed, all using small open AI models.

Source: paper, section 4

Chart · 7 of 14

Higher scores across the board

With the same small model, SwarmEvolve averaged 0.804. The rivals got 0.693, 0.642 and 0.700.

Its small-model average also beat all three rivals on a larger model: 0.804 against 0.774, 0.733 and 0.731.

Source: paper, Table 1; section 4

Chart · 8 of 14

Minutes, not hours

On some puzzles it matched what OpenEvolve reached in 60 minutes in just 2 or 3 minutes.

The striped bars are puzzles where it did not get there.

Source: paper, section 4; Figure 4, target OpenEvolve; section 4.1

The big one · 9 of 14

Many more tries per hour

In one hour it tests between 5 and 13 times as many programs as its rivals.

For the middle puzzle, matching a rival's best 60 minutes took just 3 to 8 minutes.

More tries means more ideas checked.

Source: paper, section 4.1; section 4; section 1

A median is the middle value of a list.

Contested · 11 of 14

Contested: the median hides some losses

Headline: the middle puzzle takes 3 to 8 minutes.

For rectangle packing, kissing numbers and eigenvalues, the paper lists 65 minutes.

That is longer than the 60 minutes it was given, so it reads as not reached.

On kissing numbers it scored 0.309. The rivals scored 0.481, 0.508 and 0.371.

Rectangle packing with circles: 0.888 against 0.969, 0.958 and 0.987.

On eigenvalues: 0.700 against 0.723 for each rival.

Contested. The median is true, but it sits next to three puzzles where the method scored below every rival. Read it as fast on most puzzles, not all.

Source: paper, section 1; Figure 4, target OpenEvolve; section 4.1; Table 1

12 of 14

A small model tops a famous result

With a small model, SwarmEvolve beat AlphaEvolve's published answers on three puzzles.

One example is packing 26 circles in a square, at 2.635983.

AlphaEvolve itself used much larger frontier models.

Source: paper, section 4.1

13 of 14

More helpers help early, then fade

The team tried swarms of different sizes, including 45 and 75 agents.

After 50 tries, they scored 0.64 and 0.68.

After 600 tries, every size scored 0.90.

Source: paper, section A.2; appendix tables, section A.2

Payoff · 14 of 14

What this changes

Smart search may come from many cheap minds that share memory.

One big mind writing long essays may not be the only path.

The proof so far is 21 puzzles, one preprint, and a fixed time limit.

Other teams must repeat it before we trust it.

Source: paper, section 4
